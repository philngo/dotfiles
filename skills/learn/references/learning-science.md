# Learning Science Behind `learn`

The research rationale for each rule in `SKILL.md`. Read this when a situation
isn't covered by the rules and you need to make a judgment call.

The goal of a `learn` session is a **transferable mental model**: the learner
can predict, explain, and reason about cases the session never covered.
Recall of facts is a means, not the end.

## 1. Feeling of understanding is not evidence of understanding

**Finding.** Learners (and tutors) judge learning by how fluent material feels
in the moment. Fluency is a poor predictor of later performance. Conditions
that feel slower and harder often produce better retention and transfer
("desirable difficulties"), and learners systematically prefer the strategies
that work worse. In Kornell (2009), spacing beat massing for ~90% of
participants, yet ~72% believed massing had worked better.

**Rule it drives.** Never treat "makes sense", "got it", or a nod as evidence.
Only an answer that requires the model to be used (a prediction, an
explanation of *why*, an application) counts.

Sources: Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013); Soderstrom & Bjork (2015);
Kornell (2009).

## 2. Retrieval builds the memory; re-reading doesn't

**Finding.** Practice testing produces better delayed retention than
re-studying, even when immediate performance looks worse. Dunlosky et al.'s
review rates practice testing and distributed practice as the two
highest-utility techniques; highlighting and re-reading rate low.

**Rule it drives.** Every chunk of teaching ends with the learner producing
something. Short chunks, then a question, then stop.

Sources: Roediger & Karpicke (2006); Dunlosky et al. (2013).

## 3. Attempting before being told helps, even when the attempt fails

**Finding.** Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance learning of the
answer that follows. Generating a prediction primes attention to the gap.
Giebl et al. found novice programmers who tried a problem before searching
retained more than those who searched first.

**Rule it drives.** When the learner has any relevant prior model, ask for a
prediction before revealing. Wrong predictions are useful data, not failures.

**Limit.** If the learner has no usable model at all, a forced guess is noise.
Give a worked example first (see §5).

Sources: Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009); Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009);
Giebl et al. (2021).

## 4. Errors help only with clear, corrective feedback

**Finding.** Errors followed by feedback are learned from; errors without
feedback persist. Feedback that explains *why* outperforms right/wrong
verification. High-confidence errors, once corrected, are corrected *more*
reliably than low-confidence ones (hypercorrection effect).

**Rule it drives.** When an answer is wrong, say so plainly, name the
misconception behind it, and fix that, not just the surface answer. Asking
for confidence before revealing makes hypercorrection work for you.

Sources: Butler & Roediger (2008); Shute (2008); Hattie & Timperley (2007);
Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001).

## 5. Worked examples for novices, problems for the experienced

**Finding.** For novices, studying worked examples beats unguided problem
solving because searching for a solution consumes the working memory needed
to build the schema. As expertise grows the effect reverses: examples become
redundant and slow the learner down (expertise reversal). Fading — removing
steps from successive examples until the learner solves alone — bridges the
two.

**Rule it drives.** Use the starting-point diagnostic to decide per core
idea: new to the learner → worked example first, then fade; adjacent to what
they know → prediction first. If they struggle, move back up the scaffolding
by asking a more specific question; don't hint at the answer.

Sources: Sweller & Cooper (1985); Renkl & Atkinson (2003); Kalyuga, Ayres,
Chandler & Sweller (2003); Kalyuga (2007).

## 6. Working memory is small — segment

**Finding.** Working memory holds roughly four chunks of new information.
Learner-paced segments outperform continuous presentations of the same
content.

**Rule it drives.** One idea per chunk. A chunk that introduces more than a
few new terms or moving parts is too big; split it.

Sources: Cowan (2001); Mayer & Chandler (2001).

## 7. Explaining *why* builds the model

**Finding.** Learners who explain to themselves why each step holds learn
more and transfer better than those who only read the steps. Experts
organize knowledge by underlying principles; novices by surface features.

**Rule it drives.** Teach each core idea as a mechanism (cause → effect), and
make most questions ask *why* or *what would happen if*. Research should
look for the few principles the topic follows from, not a list of facts.

Sources: Chi et al. (1989); Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981);
Dunlosky et al. (2013) on self-explanation and elaborative interrogation.

## 8. Comparing cases extracts the principle

**Finding.** Comparing two cases side by side helps learners abstract the
shared structure far better than studying each case alone. Contrasting cases
before an explanation prepare learners to understand it. Moving from
concrete examples to the abstract form ("concreteness fading") supports
transfer.

**Rule it drives.** For transfer, present a case that shares the deep
structure but differs on the surface, and ask the learner what carries over.

Sources: Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003); Schwartz & Bransford (1998);
Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014).

## 9. Spacing and interleaving within one sitting

**Finding.** Distributed practice beats massed practice. Within a single
session, what matters most is that the first retrieval is *delayed* past
other material: in Karpicke & Roediger (2007), equally spaced retrieval
beat an expanding schedule for retention two days later. Interleaving
practice across related ideas helps learners discriminate *which* idea
applies — the benefit is strongest when the ideas are easily confused, and
can reverse for unrelated material.

**Rule it drives.** After teaching idea 2, ask a question that needs idea 1.
Mix questions across core ideas without labelling which idea each one
tests, especially where ideas are easily confused.

Sources: Cepeda et al. (2006); Karpicke & Roediger (2007); Rohrer & Taylor
(2007); Brunmair & Richter (2019).

## 10. A question is only useful if it has a defensible answer

**Finding.** Ambiguous items measure confusion, not understanding, and
undermine the learner's trust in feedback. Item-writing research recommends
one clearly best answer, no undefined comparatives ("most important") without
stated criteria, and options that are plausible but unambiguously wrong.

**Rule it drives.** Every question is written with its answer and a source
before the learner sees it. Questions with more than one defensible answer
are rewritten or explicitly framed as judgment calls with criteria. When a
learner challenges a question, re-check it against the source; if it is
flawed, say so and withdraw it.

Sources: Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez (2002).

## 11. Give the map before the territory

**Finding.** Learning the names and behaviour of a system's key components
before the main lesson improves understanding of how they interact
(pre-training principle). Advance organizers, a short high-level frame given
before detailed material, have a small but reliable positive effect on
learning and retention. Pretesting helps too, but its benefit depends on
the explanation that follows; a pretest with no subsequent instruction is
just a quiz.

**Rule it drives.** Send a brief orientation (problem, core mechanisms, how
they connect) before the first teaching question. After every prediction,
state the mechanism, even when the learner got it right.

Sources: Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell (2002); Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980);
Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009).

## 12. Leading questions teach the asker's answer

**Finding.** Generation benefits come from the learner constructing the
answer from their own model. When questions are built to converge on a
conclusion the tutor already holds, the learner's work shifts from reasoning
to guessing what the tutor wants, and the tutor's conclusion is what gets
encoded. This is a design rule inferred from §3 and §7, not a single study.

**Rule it drives.** Don't build the question bank from conclusions reached
earlier in the session. Teach with neutral examples; let the learner bring
the model to their own work.
